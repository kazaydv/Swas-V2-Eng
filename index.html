He didn't think his snoring was that bad — until his wife quietly started sleeping in the guest room.

It's a more common story than most couples admit. One partner's sleep gets steadily worse, night after night, until they stop mentioning it and just start sleeping somewhere else. It rarely gets talked about as a health issue. It gets talked about as a relationship problem.

But most snoring isn't a "him" problem or a "her" problem — it's a breathing problem. When the jaw relaxes deep into sleep, the mouth falls open. Air rushes through faster and less steadily than it would through the nose, and the soft tissue at the back of the throat starts to vibrate. That vibration is the sound that ends up pushing couples into separate rooms.

Once the mouth stays gently closed, the body shifts back to nasal breathing on its own — no retraining, no effort, and no awkward conversations needed. That's the idea behind mouth-taping, a method sleep researchers have studied for years, now available as a simple overnight patch.
